    ""More music hall than misery", John Peel opined on playing "Is It Really So Strange" for the first time in session. As usual it was perspicacious enough, for it highlighted the discrepancy between received wisdom concerning the band, and what was actually there if you scratched the surface a little. For while many think of The Smiths (and Morrissey, in particular) as spokesmen for disaffected loners with non-existent love lives, via quaint but miserable tunes, this was never actually true. OK, so there were tunes like that ("Never Had No One Ever", "Last Night I Dreamt That Somebody Loved Me"), but they were far from representative. On the contrary, something quite primitive lies at the heart of The Smiths' music: revenge.

    "Hand in Glove" was not just a debut single, it was Morrissey getting his own back on a world that hitherto hadn't listened. Delicate, celibate young man with gladioli in his pocket? Well, the one abiding undercurrent of The Smiths' music is violence. So, Marr could create the most sublime melodies and hook lines, but give the band their head and they could thrash away like nobody's business ("Miserable Lie", "What She Said"). And, lyrically, violence is never far from Morrissey's world - be it the epic strains of "Suffer Little Children", the boy who is stabbed at the fairground in "Rusholme Ruffians" or the 'sweetness' who should be bludgeoned in their bed in "Bigmouth Strikes Again" - the list is endless and reaches its apogee with the glorious exhortation to 'hang the DJ' ("Panic").

    All of the aspects of the band that the ill-informed still assume to be indicative of The Smiths being brittle and mopish, were actually wielded like lead-piping looking for a suitably conformist skull to crack. Unfortunately, this side of the band has gone largely unnoticed, even (especially) by those who claim The Smiths as a major influence. Like the best of the blues, The Smiths took the desolation of life and transformed its base material into the sun that shone out of their behinds."

    "Sad, pitiful and mopey? Maybe at times, but most times Morrissey & Co. were just being honest, whatever the emotion. Heck, he even extends an invite to everyone to join him in trying something new and stop being so coy ("Ask"). How could you not be endeared to someone so sincere? Even when bemoaning sad experiences, Morrissey took himself half-seriously most of the time. How can one not chuckle just a bit when Morrissey explains that the reason he knows a certain gal doesn't like him is "because she said so" ("Girl Afraid")? But what drives the music was none other than Johnny Marr. His knack for melody and extremely tasteful delivery are second to none, and beautifully counterbalance Morrissey's dry wit and brutal honesty. Like oil and water, the two shouldn't mix. But they do so very well. They are one of the few bands where I can say with one or two exceptions, every single song they wrote is worth hearing. Multiple, multiple times." - musictoad

    "The Smiths were one of the few really important bands of the '80s. In fact, they were often seen as the antithesis of the '80s, like R.E.M. in the States, and set themselves against the big trends of the times; the synthesizers and the music videos. They had a natural retro sound that had a psychedelic dreaminess about it which fitted perfectly with Morrissey's cryptic lyrics (that for the most part read like pure poetry). The music is, despite this, very accessible with a crisp and clear sound, punctuated by Marr's jangly guitar. Its omnipresence gives the music sharpness, hinting at the darker and the more sarcastic layers of Morrissey's lyrics. The themes of abandonment, hope, bitterness, political criticism, and the constant threat of violence is beautifully encapsulated in the twisted romance of "Ask": "If it's not love - then it's the bomb (the bomb, the bomb, the bomb, the bomb, the bomb) - the bomb that will bring us together". So, despite being a timeless band, in this case (with the Cold War and all), they were clearly a product of their singular times." - steinib

    "I recall someone telling me that Smiths fans were freaks...people who just want to be eternally depressed and 'special'. So, as naive as I was at the time, I didn't have the intention of listening to their songs at all.
    One day, just as a coincidence, I was buying used CDs at a store, and the seller gave me a CD-R of their     Singles collection just for being a good customer. I gave it a shot and put the disc on my stereo, and it immediately made me jump from my bed. I remember that afternoon laughing hard alongside "Heaven Knows I'm Miserable Now", dancing the '80s way to "This Charming Man" or "How Soon Is Now?" and even shedding a tear when hearing the chorus of "There Is a Light That Never Goes Out". And I finally understood why my friend was right. Smiths fans ARE freaks because you cannot avoid being one. Their message is so relevant, even today, not only because of the lyrics (Morrissey's poetry is beyond words) but because the music is so vibrant and alive. Luminous or shadowy, it doesn't matter. There is not a single note wasted by Johnny Marr's guitar. A legacy of merely four years and four albums, but also a million compilations. Because to be a freak is nothing bad at all if you are not scared to show your feelings and thoughts...just for a moment. Just for a moment, you can enjoy being part of this world." - sergegrone

    Biography

    The Smiths, arguably one of Britain's most influential rock groups of the eighties, were founded in late 1982. Over the course of the next few months, the group acquired a fanatical following thanks to iconic frontman Morrissey and their idiosyncratic stageshows. They rejected a deal with Manchester's own Factory Records (home to Joy Division and New Order, among others), and signed with London's Rough Trade Records instead.

    Their first single "Hand in Glove" (1983) made a big impact on the British indie scene, with its allusions to same-sex love. The band's next few singles saw The Smiths climbing the pop charts, indicating that the time was ripe for the group's distinctive sound, inspired by punk and sixties pop and driven by guitars (much unlike most early '80s pop music). Johnny Marr would provide the musical background for Morrissey's lyrics, making for a fruitful creative process: Morrissey would sometimes provocatively tweak Marr's songs beyond recognition.

    Their debut, 1984's The Smiths, sold very well, setting the standard for the same year's Hatful of Hollow, a collection of BBC session tracks and B-sides.

    Meat Is Murder entered the charts at number one in early 1985, showing off Morrissey's vegetarian beliefs and his antipathy for the Thatcher government. A minor drop in popularity followed (The Smiths' aesthetic had been copied by many other contemporaries, and the novelty of guitar-driven pop quickly wore off). However, by the time of 1986's The Queen Is Dead, the group quickly regained momentum, and even managed to carve out a niche in the USA. The Queen Is Dead would originally have been named Margaret on the Guillotine - another stab at Mrs. Thatcher - but that title was rejected by the record company.

    Some signs of decline were already showing up, though: Johnny Marr was allegedly sliding into a number of bad habits, and Craig Gannon, the rhythm guitarist The Smiths had hired for their 1986 world tour, was fired as quickly as he'd been recruited. Andy Rourke, the bassist, was fired as well during this period, due to his heroin addiction; however, he re-joined The Smiths a few weeks later.

    After a couple of well-received singles (including the controversial "Panic"), another compilation, The World Won't Listen, was released in early 1987 with a further compilation, Louder Than Bombs, released in the US. Although they had arguably reached their popularity peak at that point, Morrissey and Johnny Marr, the songwriters and primary members of The Smiths, didn't get along that well anymore. Marr announced his decision to leave The Smiths in summer 1987 and, after a brief stint with guitarist Ivor Perry in Marr's stead, Morrissey subsequently jumped ship to pursue a solo career. By the time that Strangeways, Here We Come, the band's fourth and final album, was released in September of that year, The Smiths were already no more.

    Various Smiths compilations have been released over the course of the years: 1988's Rank, a live album recorded during their 1986 tour, and 1995's Singles, one of a number of best-of compilations, are maybe the most noteworthy post-breakup releases.

    Andy Rourke and The Smiths' drummer, Mike Joyce, filed a lawsuit against Marr and Morrissey in 1996, demanding an equal redistribution of performance royalties (from the 40:40:10:10 split they had been operating under). Rourke would pull out of the lawsuit and settle out of court, but Joyce was awarded nearly £1 million in back royalties and 25% going forward. Because of this and their own personal differences, Morrissey and Marr have both ruled out the possibility of a Smiths reunion.
